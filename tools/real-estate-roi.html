The four numbers that describe any rental deal

  1. Gross yield = annual rent ÷ price. The headline comparison number — but it ignores every cost, so never buy on it alone.
  2. Net yield / cap rate = net operating income ÷ price. What the asset earns regardless of financing; the number professionals quote.
  3. Cash flow = NOI − mortgage payment. The money that actually hits (or leaves) your bank account monthly.
  4. Cash-on-cash return = annual cash flow ÷ cash invested. Your personal return on the deposit, taxes and fees you put in — the figure to compare against simply investing in an index fund.

Country-specific screens the calculator applies

  • 🇺🇸 The 1% rule: monthly rent ≥ 1% of purchase price is the classic quick filter — rarely met in prime coastal markets, common in the Midwest and South. Below 1%, cash flow usually depends on big down payments or rate drops.
  • 🇬🇧 Lender stress test: buy-to-let lenders typically require rent to cover ~145% of the mortgage interest calculated at a stressed rate (~7%) — the table shows whether your deal passes.
  • 🇬🇧 Purchase costs: the 5% additional-property stamp duty surcharge is added automatically — it routinely adds five figures to the cash required.

Where paper deals die

  • Vacancy: even great properties sit empty between tenants — 5–8% of rent is a realistic allowance.
  • Maintenance: budget 1% of property value per year, more for older stock; boilers and roofs don't care about your spreadsheet.
  • Management: 8–12% of rent if you won't self-manage — include it even if you plan to, because your time isn't free.
  • Rate risk: on interest-only finance, every 1% rate rise cuts monthly cash flow by the loan × 1% ÷ 12.
FAQ

Common questions

What is a good rental yield?

Gross yields of 5–8% are typical for solid UK regional and US midwest deals; prime London or coastal US cities often run 3–4% gross and rely on appreciation. Judge net yield (cap rate) and cash-on-cash rather than gross alone.

What is the 1% rule in real estate?

A US screening heuristic: monthly rent should be at least 1% of the purchase price ($2,000 rent on a $200,000 home). Deals passing it usually cash-flow with conventional financing; it's a filter for further analysis, not a guarantee.

Should I use interest-only or repayment for the analysis?

Model what you'd actually use. UK buy-to-let is predominantly interest-only (maximising cash flow, no equity build); US investment loans are typically amortising. The calculator supports both — compare them to see the trade-off.

Does this include tax on the rental profit?

No — it measures the deal pre-tax, which is how cap rates are quoted. For the after-tax picture use the Buy-to-Let Analyzer, which applies UK Section 24 interest restriction or US Schedule E depreciation.

Is negative cash flow ever acceptable?

Only as a conscious bet on appreciation or future rent growth, with reserves to fund the monthly loss. Many experienced investors simply refuse negative-cash-flow deals: the market owes you nothing on price.
