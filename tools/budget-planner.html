The 50/30/20 rule, explained properly

Popularised by Senator Elizabeth Warren, the 50/30/20 rule divides your after-tax income into three buckets:

  • 50% Needs — housing, utilities, groceries, insurance, minimum transport. Things you genuinely can't skip.
  • 30% Wants — restaurants, streaming, hobbies, holidays, upgrades. Life's enjoyable extras.
  • 20% Savings & debt — emergency fund, pension/401(k) beyond the match, investing, and debt payments above the minimum.

It's a benchmark, not a law. In expensive cities housing alone can eat 40%+, pushing needs past 50% — the point is to know where you stand and move deliberately toward the targets.

How to fix each kind of overspend

  • Needs too high: these are the slow, high-impact fixes — renegotiate rent or remortgage, switch energy and insurance at renewal, review car costs. One good change saves money every month for years.
  • Wants too high: the fast fixes — audit subscriptions (the average household pays for several it forgot), set a weekly eating-out cap, and use a 24-hour rule on non-essential purchases.
  • Savings too low: automate a standing order on payday ("pay yourself first"), then increase it by 1% of income every few months — small enough not to notice, big enough to compound.

What to do with a surplus, in order

  1. £1,000 / $1,000 starter emergency fund.
  2. Clear expensive debt (anything above ~8% interest).
  3. Grow the emergency fund to 3–6 months of needs.
  4. Capture the full employer pension/401(k) match.
  5. Invest tax-free — ISA in the UK, Roth IRA / 401(k) in the US.
FAQ

Common questions

Should I use gross or take-home income for the 50/30/20 rule?

Take-home (after-tax) income. Tax is already gone before you can allocate anything, so the rule only makes sense applied to the money that actually reaches your account.

Are debt payments a need or savings?

Minimum payments are a need — you must make them. Anything above the minimum counts in the 20% savings-and-debt bucket, because it's building your net worth just like saving does.

What if my needs are more than 50% of income?

Common in high-cost cities. Trim wants to protect at least some saving, and work on the structural fixes — housing costs, transport, insurance renewals. Even 10% saved consistently builds real money.

How big should my emergency fund be?

Three to six months of essential spending (your needs figure, not total income). Lean toward six months if you're self-employed, the sole earner, or in a volatile industry.

Is my budget data stored anywhere?

No. The calculation runs entirely in your browser — nothing you enter is transmitted, stored or seen by anyone.
